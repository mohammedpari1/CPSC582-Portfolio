01 / EXPLORE
From interests to a visual metaphor

My early ideas centered on travel, meeting people and discovering new places. I developed the Navigator concept around an airplane-window view, blue-and-white colours, and rounded profile cards. The travel motif became a way to express identity quickly, before the user even opened the page.

02 / DECIDE
Make the interaction understandable

As a team, we considered dynamic buttons and other ways to compare members. The selected drag-two-buttons comparison gave the experience a clear purpose: help people notice both shared interests and differences.

03 / REFLECT
Identity needs both personality and clarity

I learned to connect aesthetic choices to the information they communicate. A distinctive visual theme matters, but the experience also needs an understandable path from curiosity to discovery.

THE COMPLETE DESIGN STORY
From a personal idea to a shared interaction
DISCOVER
Finding a meaningful identity

My first step was to list the things that felt most representative of me: travel, football, driving, trying new foods, being outdoors, and helping others. Travel stood out because it also connected to how I like meeting people and discovering new places. Instead of putting all those interests into a generic profile, I looked for one recognizable idea that could tie them together.

The result was Navigator—a name that suggests both exploration and helping people find direction. I wanted the button to feel personal before a visitor read a single sentence.

SKETCH
Organizing the information

My early sketches explored a profile area, personal interests, a journey section, and a team-connections area. I tried both broad page layouts and smaller card-based sections. This helped me decide what should be visible immediately and what could be discovered after interaction.

The sketches shown below come directly from my pages in the project report (pages 12–13), rather than being recreated portfolio illustrations.

DESIGN
A visual language inspired by flying

I chose the view through an airplane window, with a wing above the clouds, as the visual metaphor for my page. Blue and white create a calm atmosphere, while soft rounded cards make different parts of the profile easy to scan. Small handwritten-style phrases add warmth without making the layout feel crowded.

My page includes travel highlights, interests, and connections with teammates—for example, football and the gym with Annas, movies and food with Milad, and travel and adventure with Hafiz.

CONNECT
Make the group feel like a family

We discussed several homepage ideas, including buttons that move on their own. The team selected a simpler drag-and-drop comparison: visitors place two buttons in a shared area to discover common interests and differences. The design gives people an active way to explore relationships rather than passively reading profiles.

Our team used a shared interests table to ground these comparisons in the information we collected, rather than making up similarities.

What I would improve next

I would test the page with people who have never seen our prototype, check whether they recognize that the button is interactive, and make navigation back to the comparison page more obvious. I would also check readability and keyboard access so the interaction works for more people.

KEY TAKEAWAY

Designing for self-expression taught me to make visuals meaningful, not just attractive.

THE CHALLENGE

Help an expert teach a novice in a complex creative tool.

PROJECT CONTEXTCollaborative team concept, prototype and testing

Our team explored how voice commands could support an expert guiding a beginner through Blender. The most important design question became not just whether commands worked, but whether the interaction helped someone learn.

01 / CONSIDER ALTERNATIVES
Compare before committing

Our initial directions included shared mouse control, an AI hover assistant, and voice input. The team chose voice commands as the primary interaction, supported by a web interface and Blender add-on.

02 / ITERATE
Two modes, two different goals

“Blender does it” runs recognized actions. “Learner does it” provides shortcuts and guidance so the learner performs the action. The distinction reframed the experience from automation toward skill development.

03 / TEST & LEARN
Real interactions reveal friction

Internal testing surfaced microphone selection problems, merged rapid commands, and speech recognition errors. The prototype used a typed fallback and remained limited to English, a fixed command vocabulary, and a single-computer setup.

THE COMPLETE DESIGN STORY
Designing a tool that teaches, not just automates
THE PROBLEM
Blender is powerful—and difficult to explain

When a beginner is learning Blender, an expert can describe an action but the learner still has to locate tools, remember shortcuts, and follow a sequence of steps. Conventional video calls and screen sharing can show the interface, yet they do not necessarily help the learner practice. Our brief pushed us to explore a more embodied way for an expert to share knowledge.

CONCEPT SELECTION
Why we chose voice

We considered an AI hover assistant, where pointing at an object would reveal guidance, and shared mouse control, where both people could manipulate the same scene. We set aside the AI assistant because the assignment centered on a human expert; shared control risked becoming confusing. Voice commands kept the expert in the loop and let the learner focus on the model.

PROTOTYPE V1
“Blender does it”

In the first version, the expert presses Start and speaks commands such as “cube move up” or “add a red sphere.” The browser recognizes the speech, and a Blender add-on applies supported actions. The interface shows what was heard and what happened, making the result visible. The team also designed useful shortcuts in the language itself: “it” can refer to the last-mentioned object, commands can use default amounts, and “undo” reverses an action.

TESTING
Small breakdowns, important insights

Internal tests uncovered several issues. The browser sometimes selected the wrong microphone. Speaking too quickly could merge commands. Noise and misheard words could make voice input unreliable. The team improved run instructions, displayed command feedback, added a typed fallback, and mapped some common misrecognitions. When an object did not exist, the system was changed to reject the command rather than act on the wrong object.

PROTOTYPE V2
“Learner does it”

Our biggest conceptual change came when we realized that automation alone did not teach the learner how to use Blender. In the second mode, the expert can say the same command, but the software provides on-screen guidance instead of executing it. For example, moving a cube upward prompts the learner to use G → Z → distance → Enter. The learner performs the action and can switch back to demonstration mode to compare.

FEEDBACK DESIGN
Keep attention near the model

The team placed instructions inside the Blender viewport so the learner would not need to keep looking away. Different text colours distinguish the recognized instruction, the result or “Your turn,” and the keyboard steps. The message disappears after roughly 12 seconds, while recent commands remain available in a panel. This balances immediate guidance with a less cluttered workspace.

What the prototype still cannot do

It was demonstrated with the expert page and Blender on one computer rather than a true two-computer remote connection. It supports English and a fixed command set, does not verify that the learner pressed the correct keys, assumes default Blender shortcuts, and has not been tested with actual beginners. These are clear priorities for a future iteration—not claims of features already built.

My design learning

The strongest lesson I take from this project is that efficiency and learning are not identical. Making a command happen instantly is useful for demonstration; helping a beginner perform it independently requires a different interaction. Testing the real experience, not just whether the technology runs, is what makes the design stronger.

KEY TAKEAWAY

A faster interface is not always a better learning experience. The user's goal should shape the interaction.

03 / DESIGN PHILOSOPHY

THE PRINCIPLES I AM DEVELOPING

Good design begins with understanding.

I believe an interaction should be more than visually appealing or technically impressive. It should help people understand what they can do—and feel confident doing it.

01

Make meaning visible.

Use form, language and feedback to communicate purpose. My Navigator concept taught me how visual identity can guide understanding.

02

Explore more than one answer.

Sketching and concept comparisons reveal tradeoffs that can be easy to miss when I commit to the first idea.

03

Design for the actual goal.

The Blender project showed me that completing an action for someone and helping them learn an action are different outcomes.

04

Keep asking what could improve.

Testing, limitations and critique are evidence for the next iteration—not simply a checklist at the end.

04 / REFLECTION

WHAT CHANGED IN MY THINKING

Not just better screens.
Better questions.

STARTING POINT

How can I express an idea?

In early exercises and Family of Buttons, I focused on concept generation, personality, metaphors and visual clarity.

THROUGH THE PROJECTS

How does the interaction change behavior?

With Blender, I saw how a design can make a task easy while leaving the learner passive. The difference between doing and teaching became a meaningful design consideration.

LOOKING AHEAD

How will I know it works for people?

My next step is to test with people outside my team, observe moments of confusion, and make design changes based on evidence rather than assumptions.

MY DESIGN DIRECTION

Stay curious. Design with purpose.
Learn from every iteration.
